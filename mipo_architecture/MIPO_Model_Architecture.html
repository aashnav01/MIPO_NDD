How MIPO turns one mutation into a score

MIPO predicts what a lab experiment would measure for a single amino-acid change in a protein it was never trained on. It reads the protein with a frozen language model, drops a signal on the mutated residue, lets that signal spread to nearby residues in 3D, and converts the result into a score for that specific experiment, together with how unsure it is.

Stage 1 · Input · learned numbers: 0 (frozen)

Protein sequence

Goes in
The PTEN amino-acid sequence, 403 residues.
What it does
ESM-2 (650M), a protein language model built by others, reads the whole protein once and writes 1,280 numbers describing every residue. Long proteins are read in overlapping windows of 768. The results are saved to disk, so this is not repeated for each variant. MIPO never changes ESM-2.
Comes out
403 × 1,280 numbers, saved to disk.

Stage 1 · Input · learned numbers: 0

The mutation

Goes in
Which residue changes, and to what: C124S.
What it does
Nothing is computed here. It only names the position (residue 124 of the reference sequence used in the experiment) and the two amino acids, cysteine before and serine after.
Comes out
Position 124, from C, to S.

Stage 1 · Input · learned numbers: 0

3D structure

Goes in
Coordinates of each residue in 3D, plus a confidence between 0 and 1 for each residue.
What it does
PTEN has one structure (up to 4 are allowed). The confidence at residue 124 is 0.94. Residues with confidence 0.5 or lower are ignored when measuring distances and directions. If a protein has no structure, MIPO uses chain neighbours only and never invents coordinates.
Comes out
Coordinates and confidence, passed to the residue graph.

Stage 2 · Prepare · learned numbers: 164,224

Residue context

Goes in
Each residue's 1,280 ESM-2 numbers.
What it does
One learned layer shrinks 1,280 numbers to 128 (then evens them out). This 128-number context is what later parts use to decide which residues matter to each other. The same layer also shrinks the whole-protein average.
Comes out
128 numbers for every residue.

Stage 2 · Prepare · learned numbers: 37,088

Mutation impulse

Goes in
The two amino acids, C and S, plus ESM-2's description of residue 124.
What it does
Compares C and S two ways: a learned 24-number fingerprint per amino acid, and four fixed chemistry numbers (hydropathy, volume, charge, polar side chain; for C124S: −3.3, −19.5, 0, +1 before scaling). That is 28 numbers of difference. A learned layer turns them into 128 numbers between −1 and 1, and a gate computed from residue 124's context sets how strong the signal is. The signal is placed on residue 124; every other residue starts at zero.
Comes out
A 128-number signal on residue 124, zeros elsewhere.

Stage 2 · Prepare · learned numbers: 0

Residue graph

Goes in
The structure and the mutated position.
What it does
Chooses 256 of PTEN's 403 residues: 51 around the mutation, 19 close to it in 3D, and 186 evenly spaced. Gives each residue 16 links (up to 8 nearest in 3D, the rest nearest along the chain). Puts 5 numbers on every link: distance, how much the distance varies between structures, contact strength, gap along the chain, and confidence. This is ordinary code on the CPU, run for every variant. Nothing is learned.
Comes out
A graph of 256 residues with 16 links each.

Stage 3 · Spread · learned numbers: 304,566

Response field

Goes in
The impulse, the graph, and every residue's context.
What it does
Three rounds. In each round, every residue collects three things. (1) Neighbour messages: a small network scores each of its 16 links from both residues' context and the 5 link numbers; the scores become weights that add to 1, and the neighbours' values are mixed with them. (2) 3D direction: arrows to its linked residues are added up and only the length is kept, so rotating the protein changes nothing. (3) 16 hubs: residues are softly assigned to 16 landmarks that pool their members and pass the result back, so distant residues hear each other in one step. The residue then updates: new = old + step × change. The step size is learned and starts near 0.27. There are no constant terms, so an all-zero field stays zero.
Comes out
128 numbers on every kept residue: the response field. Each round has its own copy of the layers (about 101,500 learned numbers each).

Stage 4 · Summarise · learned numbers: 271,488

Sequence summaries

Goes in
ESM-2's numbers for the 51 residues around 124 (99 to 149), and the whole-protein average.
What it does
Window reader: shrinks each residue to 128, adds a learned tag for its slot (−25 to +25), and runs 2 transformer layers with 4 attention heads each, so every residue in the window looks at all the others. Only the centre output, residue 124, is kept. The whole-protein average goes through the same shrinking layer as Residue context.
Comes out
Two 128-number lists, used as views ① and ③.

Stage 4 · Input · learned numbers: 16,896

Zero-shot LLR

Goes in
ESM-2's probabilities for residue 124 when 124 is hidden (computed once and saved).
What it does
LLR = log p(S) − log p(C) = −12.09 for C124S. Divided by 10 gives −1.209. A second number says the LLR is present (1). A small network turns [−1.209, 1] into 128 numbers.
Comes out
128 numbers, added to the impulse for view ④.

Stage 4 · Summarise · learned numbers: 0

Four views

Goes in
Everything from the boxes above.
What it does
Nothing new is computed; it lines up four lists of 128 numbers so they can be weighed. ① Site: window reader output plus the field at residue 124 (what is around it and what happened to it). ② Field average over all 256 kept residues (the overall effect). ③ Whole-protein average (which protein this is). ④ Impulse plus LLR (the change itself and ESM-2's opinion of it).
Comes out
4 × 128 numbers.

Stage 5 · Mix · learned numbers: 230,532

Weighted mix

Goes in
The four views, plus the structure's confidence at residue 124.
What it does
A small network reads all four views (513 numbers) and outputs four weights that add to 1: which views to trust for this variant. Each view is multiplied by its weight, and a second network (512 to 256 to 128) mixes them. During training, views ① to ③ are switched off at random 10% of the time so the model cannot rely on one source. View ④ is never switched off.
Comes out
128 numbers, and the 4 weights.

Stage 5 · Input · learned numbers: 2,248

Assay description

Goes in
The task (activity, stability, abundance, binding, fitness or consensus) and details such as host, selection, system, treatment and protein region.
What it does
The task becomes a learned list of 24 numbers. The details become a learned list of 16, averaged over the items. Joined, that is a 40-number code. Gene names and assay IDs are never used. During training, 15% of variants hide their task, so the model also learns a general code for assay types it has not seen.
Comes out
A 40-number code.

Stage 6 · Squeeze · learned numbers: 17,544

Bottleneck

Goes in
The 128 mixed numbers.
What it does
A small network (128 to 128 to 8) followed by tanh forces everything through 8 numbers between −1 and 1. The 8 numbers have no names, and every kind of assay reads from the same 8.
Comes out
8 numbers.

Stage 6 · Weights · learned numbers: 3,794

Hypernetwork

Goes in
The 40-number assay code.
What it does
A network that writes the weights of another network. It maps 40 to 64 to 18 numbers. The 18 are, for each of two outputs (score and uncertainty), 8 weights and 1 offset. So the same 8 numbers are read differently for an activity assay than for an abundance assay. If the task is unknown, the code asks for a general readout.
Comes out
18 numbers: the readout's weights.

Stage 7 · Read out · learned numbers: 0 (weights come from the hypernetwork)

Readout

Goes in
The 8 bottleneck numbers and the 18 weights.
What it does
μ = (8 weights × 8 numbers, added up) ÷ √8 + offset. σ = softplus(second output) + 0.05, so it is always positive and at least 0.05. Both are on the training scale (each assay rescaled with the training genes' median and spread), and are converted back to the assay's own units afterwards.
Comes out
Score μ and uncertainty σ.

Stage 8 · Output · learned numbers: 0

Prediction

Goes in
μ and σ for every variant of the test gene.
What it does
Saved per variant with the real measurement next to it, in test_predictions.csv (12,343 rows for PTEN). A 90% range is μ ± 1.645 σ. Spearman per assay goes to test_metrics.csv, and the gene's headline score to test_summary.json.
Comes out
The files above, written by every training run.

Stage 8 · Output (optional) · learned numbers: 0

Effect map

Goes in
A saved checkpoint and a list of variants.
What it does
Run mipo predict --export-fields. It saves the response field from stage 3 (128 numbers per kept residue) and the 3D direction vectors for each variant. Training does not save these.
Comes out
field_*.npz files.

Stage 8 · Output (optional) · learned numbers: 0

What it leaned on

Goes in
The same command as the effect map.
What it does
Also saves the 4 view weights from stage 5 and the 8 bottleneck numbers from stage 6, so you can see which sources counted for each variant.
Comes out
The same field_*.npz files.

Words used above

  • MLP: a small network of two learned layers with a squashing step between them. MIPO has 9 of them.
  • Attention: a step where each item scores the others, the scores become weights that add to 1, and the items are mixed with those weights. MIPO uses it in the window reader (2 transformer layers × 4 heads = 8 heads) and in a lighter form 7 times: the neighbour weights in each of the 3 rounds, the hub assignment in each of the 3 rounds, and the four view weights.
  • tanh: squashes any number into the range −1 to 1.
  • Embedding: a learned list of numbers that stands for a category, such as an amino acid or a task.

Stage 2 in detail: PTEN's residue graph for C124S

These are the real numbers MIPO computes for C124S, from PTEN's structure in your release. PTEN is too long to use every residue, so MIPO keeps 256 of the 403, chosen in a fixed order until the budget is full.

Stage 3 in detail: one round of spreading

This is where MIPO differs most from a plain model on ESM-2 features. In each round every kept residue updates its 128 numbers from three sources at once.

Step by step, in plain words

  1. Read the protein

    ESM-2 is a protein language model trained by others on millions of sequences. MIPO uses it as a fixed reader: it describes every residue of PTEN with 1,280 numbers, and MIPO never changes ESM-2 itself. MIPO shrinks each description to 128 numbers and also keeps the average over the whole protein.

    ESM-2 t33 650M, cached once per exact sequence. Long proteins are read in 768-residue windows with 128 overlap. Projection: Linear 1,280 → 128 + LayerNorm.

  2. Describe the mutation

    MIPO compares serine with cysteine in two ways. It has a learned 24-number fingerprint for each amino acid, and it has four fixed chemistry numbers. For C124S the chemistry changes like this:

    PropertyCysteineSerineChange
    Hydropathy (Kyte–Doolittle)2.5−0.8−3.3
    Side-chain volume (Å³)108.589.0−19.5
    Charge at neutral pH000
    Polar side chainnoyes+1

    These 28 differences become a 128-number impulse. How strong it is depends on what ESM-2 says about residue 124. The impulse sits on residue 124 only; every other residue starts at zero.

    impulse = tanh(W·[Δ fingerprint (24), Δ chemistry (4)]) × sigmoid(MLP(context of 124)). W has no bias term. Chemistry is scaled (hydropathy ÷ 5, volume ÷ 200); these are descriptors, not energies.

  3. Build the residue graph

    For PTEN C124S, MIPO keeps 256 residues: 51 around the mutation, 19 more that are close to it in 3D, and 186 evenly spaced ones (Figure 2). Each kept residue gets 16 links: up to 8 nearest in 3D, using only confidently placed residues, and the rest nearest along the chain. Every link carries five numbers.

    Node budget 256; confidence threshold 0.5; up to 4 structures (PTEN has 1). Direction arrows only for confident pairs closer than 16 Å. Missing structure → chain links only, never invented coordinates. Built on the CPU for every variant.

  4. Spread the effect, three times

    In each round, every residue updates its value from its linked residues, from a 3D direction signal, and from 16 shared hubs (Figure 3). After three rounds the values form the response field: MIPO's guess of how much each kept residue is affected.

    new = old + sigmoid(step) × tanh(U·[old, neighbour mix, hub message, 3D length]). Hubs cost residues × 16, not residues².

  5. Summarise four ways

    ① the site: a small transformer reads the 25 residues on each side of 124, plus the field value at 124; ② the field averaged over all kept residues; ③ the whole-protein average from step 1; ④ the impulse plus the zero-shot LLR, ESM-2's own guess of how likely serine is at 124 compared with cysteine when residue 124 is hidden.

    Window reader: 2 transformer layers, 4 heads, relative positions only. LLR = log p(S) − log p(C) with 124 masked, scaled ÷ 10, plus a "present" flag, → MLP → 128.

  6. Mix the four views

    A small network gives each view a weight; it also looks at how confident the structure is at residue 124. During training, views ① to ③ are randomly switched off 10% of the time, so the model cannot lean on a single source. View ④ is always kept.

    Gates: softmax over 4 from MLP(4 × 128 + 1). Mixing: MLP 512 → 256 → 128.

  7. Squeeze to 8 numbers

    The mix is squeezed to 8 numbers between −1 and 1. They have no names; they are a compact description of what the mutation does, and every kind of assay reads from the same 8 numbers.

    MLP 128 → 128 → 8, then tanh. The 8 coordinates are not labelled biology (folding, binding, …); the data cannot identify that.

  8. Read out for this assay

    MIPO turns the assay description into a 40-number code, and the hypernetwork turns that code into the weights of the final readout, which gives μ and σ. Gene names and assay IDs are never inputs. During training, 15% of variants hide their task, so the model also learns a general readout for assay types it has not seen.

    Code = task embedding (24) + mean of metadata embeddings (16). Hypernetwork MLP 40 → 64 → 18 = weights and bias for μ and σ. μ = w·z / √8 + b; σ = softplus(·) + 0.05.

How it learns

What each prediction is scored on

  • 1Fit the score and be honest about uncertainty. Gaussian likelihood: being wrong while claiming a small σ costs the most.
  • 0.1Limit the pull of outliers. Huber error on the score.
  • 0.2Get the order right. For pairs of variants from the same assay, the higher measured one should get the higher prediction. This matches the Spearman correlation used to judge the model.
  • 1e-5Keep the field small. A tiny penalty on the size of the response field.
  • 0.1Physical proxies (contact change, flexibility): built in, but inactive in this run because no paired mutant structures were supplied.

Training recipe in this run

Batch
8 variants from one assay (pick a task, then a gene, then one of its assays)
Update
every 4 batches, so 32 variants
Epoch
3,000 batches = 24,000 variant draws
Length
up to 20 epochs; stop after 6 without improvement
Optimiser
AdamW, learning rate 0.0003 easing to 0.000015, gradients clipped at 1
Precision
full 32-bit; mixed precision collapsed the predictions on the DGX (fold 4)
Best epoch
highest validation Spearman: within each assay, averaged per gene, then across the 5 validation genes

Where the model sits in your run

Each of the 10 folds holds out one test gene (CYP2C9, GCK, HLA-A, KCNE1, KCNJ2, KRAS, PTEN, SLC22A1, SRC or VKORC1). The model trains on 481 genes, picks its best epoch on 5 validation genes, sets its uncertainty ranges on 3 calibration genes, and is scored once on the test gene. Four models × three seeds × ten folds make 120 training runs.

Stage 2's graph is built on the CPU for every variant, and the GPU finishes its part of a batch in about 0.03 seconds. That is why the GPU sits mostly idle and why several runs share one GPU at the same time.

Two test genes have a close relative among the training genes: KRAS (HRAS, 86% identical) and SRC (FYN, 77%). Their scores partly reflect transfer within a protein family. The other eight test genes have no close relative in training.
